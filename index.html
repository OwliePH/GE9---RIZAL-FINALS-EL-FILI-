The Central Question

When people are oppressed, is it better to seek reform — or demand revolution?

Rizal wrote El Filibusterismo as a darker answer to Noli Me Tangere. Where the first novel planted seeds of hope, the second watched them burn. Below, you will choose a side — and the novel will make its case.

You chose — Reform

The Case for Peaceful Change

QUESTION 01 — REFORM VS. REVOLUTION

What does the novel say about reform as a path forward?

Rizal does not celebrate reform — he mourns its failure. Characters like Basilio and Isagani believe in education and petition, yet the Spanish colonial system crushes every gesture of good faith. The ilustrados are mocked, ignored, or arrested. Rizal shows us that reform was the right impulse, but it was made impossible by those in power. The tragedy is not that reform was wrong — it's that the oppressor never allowed it to work.

"Kabataan ang pag-asa ng bayan." — Rizal's earlier belief, now tested against the reality of El Fili's world.

QUESTION 02 — THE OPPRESSED VS. THOSE IN POWER

What do the actions of the oppressed reveal, compared to those in power?

The oppressed — students, farmers, the poor — act from desperation and dignity. They want schools, fair treatment, a voice. Those in power — friars, colonial officials — act from greed and fear of losing control. Padre Salvi, Padre Irene, and the guardia civil use religion and law as weapons. The contrast reveals a society where institutions exist not to serve people, but to preserve the privilege of the few.

The students' petition for a Spanish academy — a humble, peaceful ask — is sabotaged from within by those who feared even literacy as a threat.

QUESTION 03 — EL FILI VS. NOLI ME TANGERE

How is El Filibusterismo different from Noli Me Tangere in tone and message?

Noli Me Tangere was written in hope — Crisostomo Ibarra believed society could change. El Filibusterismo is written in grief and fury. Ibarra returns as Simoun, hollowed out, convinced that the only language power understands is destruction. Where Noli diagnosed the wound, El Fili shows what happens when it goes untreated. Rizal's message sharpened: a society that refuses reform invites revolution.

The shift in tone mirrors Rizal's own disillusionment after years of exile, censorship, and watching his countrymen suffer with no relief in sight.

Rizal's verdict: Reform was the nobler path — but a colonial power that rejects every peaceful overture leaves a people with no road left to walk.

The Case for Tearing It All Down

QUESTION 01 — REFORM VS. REVOLUTION

What does the novel say about revolution as a response to oppression?

Simoun IS the revolution — and Rizal renders him with terrifying complexity. His logic is airtight: every reform was rejected, every petition ignored, every peaceful Filipino punished. He concludes that only catastrophe can break the machine. Yet Rizal also shows Simoun's plan collapse — not because revolution is wrong, but because a revolution built on personal vengeance rather than collective love cannot succeed. The failure is Simoun's, not the idea's.

"I am not the criminal here. The criminal is the one who created the necessity for what I do." — Simoun's moral logic, disturbing in its clarity.

QUESTION 02 — THE OPPRESSED VS. THOSE IN POWER

What do the actions of those in power reveal about why revolution becomes inevitable?

The colonial authorities in El Fili are not merely corrupt — they are actively invested in keeping Filipinos ignorant and divided. The friars block education. The government fabricates sedition charges. Even well-meaning Spaniards are trapped in a system that rewards cruelty. The oppressed have no courts, no press, no vote, no recourse. When every door is locked, revolution stops being a choice and becomes the only remaining wall to break through.

The pansitería scene: Filipinos debating their future while surrounded by informants. The system surveils even the act of thinking freely.

QUESTION 03 — EL FILI VS. NOLI ME TANGERE

How does El Fili's darker tone change what Rizal is saying?

Noli Me Tangere asked: Can you see what is wrong with this society? El Filibusterismo asks: Now that you see it — what are you willing to do? The tone shifts from awakening to reckoning. Rizal removes the comforting characters, dims the light, and places the reader inside a world so broken that Simoun's bombs feel like a logical endpoint. The message: inaction in the face of injustice is its own kind of violence.

Father Florentino's final words to the dying Simoun are the novel's true thesis — do not sacrifice a people for one man's revenge, but do not ask the oppressed to simply endure forever.

Rizal's verdict: Revolution born of hatred destroys its bearer. But a people driven to revolution by centuries of injustice? That verdict falls on those who refused to listen.

Two Novels, One Unfinished Revolution

Noli Me Tangere · 1887

Tone

Hopeful, satirical, romantic — the wound is named but healing still feels possible.

Protagonist

Crisostomo Ibarra — idealistic, educated, believes in the system.

Message

Expose the cancer of colonial society. Awaken the sleeping nation.

Color

Gold and daylight — dawn breaking over a suffering land.

Question Asked

"Can you see what is destroying us?"

El Filibusterismo · 1891

Tone

Dark, bitter, tragic — the wound has festered; the patient is near death.

Protagonist

Simoun (Ibarra reborn) — cynical, vengeful, done waiting for justice.

Message

A society that ignores its wounds invites its own destruction.

Color

Red and shadow — fire consuming what daylight failed to heal.

Question Asked

"Now that you see it — what will you sacrifice?"

Where you do not have love, do not venture, for you will only accomplish evil.

— Father Florentino, El Filibusterismo

Rizal did not choose reform or revolution for us. He chose truth — that neither path succeeds without love for one's people as its foundation. Simoun failed because his revolution was built on hatred. The reformists failed because power refused to listen. What Rizal left behind was not an answer, but a mirror — and a dare to look into it.
